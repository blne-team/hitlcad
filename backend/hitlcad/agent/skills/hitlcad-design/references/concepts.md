# Exploring concepts

Use when the brief leaves room for different solutions. The output is a small set of meaningfully
different directions and a reasoned choice, scaled to the task and the human's time.

## Find the decisions with consequences

For an open brief, a useful brainstorming prompt is: “Identify the intended use, hard constraints
and open choices. Explore distinct layouts or construction strategies, then describe how different
form and style directions would express them. Compare the benefits, compromises and unknowns, and
recommend a direction against the user's priorities.” Treat this as a prompt pattern, not a
requirement to ask the human another question or create alternatives for every edit.

Intent concerns what the object must do and how it will be used. Style concerns its visual and
material expression. Brainstorm them together where useful, but retain their different roles:
a style preference does not silently change required access, mounting geometry or performance.

List the functions before naming parts: contain, support, move, locate, protect, control, cool,
connect, assemble or service. Identify constraints that dominate the layout: a bought component,
mounting interface, human reach, removal direction or manufacturing envelope.

For an enclosure, possible axes of exploration include a shell versus a frame with panels, front
versus side service access, and a single volume versus separated functional zones. Compare concepts
with the same contents and requirements. A smaller empty box is not a better solution to a brief
that requires particular contents.

Change one or two consequential decisions per concept so the tradeoff remains understandable.
Colour-only alternatives are appropriate for a colour request; spatial exploration needs different
forms, arrangements or construction. Do not force several alternatives for an already chosen design.

## Use analogies deliberately

Borrow the principle behind a reference: a building's service corridor suggests a component removal
path; a repeated bay suggests interchangeable panels; a vector symbol suggests a reusable vent motif.
Explain what transfers and what does not. A timber joint adapted to printed polymer needs its own
fit and load assessment.

Use references with different constructions and scales. Extract relationships before copying visible
features. Research on design fixation gives reason to check whether an example has narrowed the
alternatives; it does not prove a particular prompting method improves our agents.

## Compare before detailing

For each direction, state its main benefit, main compromise, unknowns and cheapest useful evaluation.
A rough envelope can answer packaging; a sectional skeleton can answer access; a motion preview can
expose obstruction. Do not use decorative detail to conceal an unresolved layout.

Choose against the human's priorities. When continuing autonomously, state the chosen direction and
why. When their preference is essential, present concrete alternatives and continue independent work
that does not depend on that preference. Stop exploring when further alternatives are unlikely to
change the decision.

## Sources and adaptation

The [Design Council's Double Diamond](https://www.designcouncil.org.uk/resources/the-double-diamond/)
describes understanding and defining a problem, developing alternatives and testing solutions. Our
envelope comparisons and CAD workflow above are applications of that process.

[Viswanathan, Design fixation to examples (2017)](https://iced23.designsociety.org/publication/39836/Design%2Bfixation%2Bto%2Bexamples%3A%2BA%2Bstudy%2Bon%2Bthe%2Btime%2Bdecay%2Bof%2Bfixation)
reports persistent example-related fixation in its engineering design experiment. Treat it as
motivation for diverse references and behavioral evaluation, not an agent benchmark result.
